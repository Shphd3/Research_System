\begin{abstract}

Large language models trained on human language exhibit strong anisotropy: a small number of directions concentrate most energy, while the remaining dimensions form a long tail. This geometry inflates dynamic range, creates extreme activation outliers, and destabilizes low-bit training, where dominant spike directions hijack quantization scale. Existing mitigation strategies rely on explicit spectral decompositions such as SVD, which are computationally expensive, memory-intensive, and poorly aligned with modern accelerator hardware.

We show that this anisotropy arises from a fundamental inductive bias of language modeling: token meaning is determined by context. Self-attention and residual accumulation reinforce shared semantic components, which accumulate into coherent mean directions. This mean bias dominates both elementwise outliers and second-moment inflation.

Crucially, because the dominant instability is rank-one, it can be removed by a simple mean-subtraction operation. This bias-centric conditioning achieves the core benefit of spectral control without costly decompositions, providing a hardware-friendly path to stable low-bit quantization and adaptive optimization while preserving semantic structure.

\end{abstract}
