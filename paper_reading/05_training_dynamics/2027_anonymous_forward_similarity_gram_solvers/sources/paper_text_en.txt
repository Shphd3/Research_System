011                            ABSTRACT
012
013            Changing the numerical precision of a differentiable Gram solver may leave pre-
014               dictions nearly unchanged while altering the computation used to learn its param-
015                eters. We study this distinction in controlled Spectral Koopman Attention (SKA)
016            and an independent episodic ridge classifier, assigning FP32 or FP64 to separate
017              solver stages without changing their exact-arithmetic definitions. Matched com-
018              parisons fix the model, input, and optimizer state; separately trained endpoints are
                 evaluated through a common precision route. For SKA, we compare q110 with the019
                 higher-precision solver route q111, a numerical reference rather than mathematical
020
               ground truth. Across 18 matched SKA validation comparisons from three training
021
                  seeds, median relative discrepancies in logits, full-model gradients, and restored
022         AdamW updates are 0.962%, 8.61%, and 9.88%, respectively, each normalized
023            by its corresponding q111 reference norm.  Prediction agreement can therefore
024             miss differences in the learning computation. In a seven-seed matched classifier
025             comparison, imposing externally prescribed ridge schedules shared across routes
026              within each seed during training reduces the absolute between-route spread in
027               post-training regularized-Gram conditioning by 97.4% relative to learning ridge
028              separately under each route. This shows that the observed conditioning separa-
029               tion depends on the training-time ridge policy. Precision choices in these solvers
                  therefore warrant checks of parameter updates alongside predictions, with end-030
                 point conditioning and task outcomes assessed separately.

034    1  INTRODUCTION
035
036     Nearly unchanged logits or validation loss can make a lower-precision solver appear interchangeable
037     with a higher-precision implementation. A solver inside an end-to-end learned model, however, also
038     supplies derivatives used by the optimizer (Amos & Kolter, 2017; Agrawal et al., 2019; Seeger
039      et al., 2017). Prediction agreement leaves the derivative computation untested: similar outputs do
040     not establish that the two implementations produce similar parameter updates.
041     Numerical sensitivity of solvers and their derivatives is established (Higham & Mary, 2022; Hov-
042     land & H¨uckelheim, 2024).  For training-time precision substitutions, however, derivative sensi-
043      tivity alone does not quantify optimizer-update discrepancies or differences retained after training.
044   We therefore separate two questions: how much do precision settings change updates at fixed pa-
045     rameters, inputs, and optimizer state, and which learned-state differences remain under a common
046     evaluation implementation? Matched parameter changes assess local training equivalence, with
047     full-model gradients as an intermediate diagnostic. Evaluating trained models through a common
048     solver implementation separates training-history differences from immediate evaluation arithmetic.
049   We study a language model using controlled Spectral Koopman Attention (SKA) (Sridhar & Jo-
050     hansen, 2026) and an independent episodic ridge classifier (Bertinetto et al., 2019). Both form a ma-
051      trix from learned feature inner products, add positive regularization, and solve a linear system. We
052     define a precision route by assigning FP32 or FP64 separately to statistic construction, factorization,
053    and solution-side computation. Within each solver, these assignments preserve the exact-arithmetic
      map while changing its numerical implementation.

075     Precision placement can change parameter updates despite close predictions, while the fixed-state
076     dual control shows that equivalent reformulation can reduce the measured sensitivity. Separately
077     trained endpoints exhibit regime-dependent differences, and ridge interventions change conditioning
078     without establishing a task benefit. The experiments distinguish the effects of numerical implemen-
079      tation on updates, learned-state statistics, and task outcomes: prediction agreement alone does not
080      validate a precision substitution for training.

082    2  BACKGROUND
083
084     Let Z ∈Rn×r contain learned features as rows and let C ∈Rr×k be the right-hand side of a linear
085     system. The regularized Gram solve obtains W ∈Rr×k from GλW = C, where Gλ = Z⊤Z +λIr
086    and λ > 0. We call λ the ridge parameter.  In exact arithmetic, it makes Gλ positive definite,
087     allowing Cholesky factorization and triangular solves. Higher-precision factorization cannot recon-
         struct information already lost during Gram construction. Precision must therefore be specified by088
        computational role (Higham & Mary, 2022).
089
090     Differentiating the exact system gives
091                     dW = G−1λ   (︁ dC −(dGλ)W )︁ ,
092    where d denotes a differential and inverse notation denotes a solve. This identity is not a finite-
093     precision error bound (Appendix C). The model gradient depends on the gradient from the down-
094     stream loss, and the parameter update depends on the optimizer’s stored state. Forward agreement
095     alone does not establish agreement of these computations (Hovland & H¨uckelheim, 2024).

190    4  EXPERIMENTS
191
192   We evaluate precision substitutions at two levels: the computation of a single update and the out-
193    comes of separate training runs. Matched comparisons hold model parameters, inputs, and optimizer
194      state fixed; separately trained models are evaluated through a common solver route. Ridge interven-
195      tions then examine how regularization affects conditioning and whether learning it online benefits
196     the task, treating these as separate questions.

443    5  RELATED WORK
444
445     Differentiable solvers and learned linear operators.   Differentiable optimization and linear-
446     algebra layers embed structured solves in end-to-end learning (Amos & Kolter, 2017; Agrawal et al.,
447     2019; Blondel et al., 2022; Ionescu et al., 2015; Seeger et al., 2017). Bertinetto et al. (2019) combine
448     episodic ridge regression with learned features and solver hyperparameters, use Woodbury’s iden-
449      tity for smaller solves, and examine the task value of learning ridge. Our classifier studies precision
450      sensitivity in this construction. Our controlled SKA derives from Echo (Sridhar & Johansen, 2026),
451     within work on finite-dimensional Koopman approximations and learned dynamical representations
452     (Williams et al., 2015; Takeishi et al., 2017; Lusch et al., 2018).
453
454     Finite-precision numerical linear algebra.  Cholesky-based QR analysis and mixed-precision
455     solvers address conditioning, roundoff, and precision allocation (Yamamoto et al., 2015; Carson
456   & Higham, 2018; Higham & Pranesh, 2021; Higham & Mary, 2022). Mixed-precision training uses
457     higher-precision accumulation, master weights, loss scaling, and tensor-level assignments (Micike-
458     vicius et al., 2018; Kalamkar et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2023). We compare solver-stage precision
459     choices through predictions, full-model gradients, and restored updates at matched parameters, in-
         puts, and optimizer state.460
461
462    Forward–backward discrepancies in training.  Automatic differentiation faces numerical pit-
463       falls, including incompletely converged iterative solvers (H¨uckelheim et al., 2024; Akbarzadeh et al.,
464     2020). Murray (2016) derives Cholesky differentiation rules; Hovland & H¨uckelheim (2024) com-
465     pare differentiating iterative solver implementations with differentiating the underlying equations,
        primarily in forward mode. Matrix-decomposition methods also adjust backward computations for466
          stability (Wang et al., 2019; 2022; Song et al., 2021). Our fixed-forward diagnostic tests solver-
467
         derivative sensitivity.  Common-route evaluation separates endpoint differences from evaluation
468
         arithmetic; ridge interventions distinguish conditioning effects from the task value of online adapta-
469      tion.

472    6  CONCLUSION
473
474     Forward agreement alone does not establish local training equivalence in differentiable Gram
475      solvers.  Matched SKA comparisons, including three additional training seeds, show close pre-
476     dictions alongside different gradients and actual parameter updates. The stage-specific pattern also
477     appears in the fixed-checkpoint CPU/A100 control, while recomputing solver derivatives improves
478     agreement without changing prediction outputs. In the separate classifier control, a standard equiva-
479      lent dual formulation substantially reduces the measured discrepancies: their magnitude is not fixed
480    by the mathematical problem alone.
481     After training, ridge and conditioning separation vary across regimes. Sharing a reference ridge
482     schedule during classifier training substantially reduces the measured between-route conditioning
483     spread; lower conditioning under a separate locked schedule does not establish better or equivalent
484     task performance.  Precision substitutions therefore need update checks in addition to prediction
485     checks, and trained models require separate evaluation of conditioning and task outcomes. These
         findings concern the two tested Gram solvers and their evaluated regimes.
